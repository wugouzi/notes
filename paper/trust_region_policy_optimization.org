* Introduction
        Most algorithms for policy optimization can be classified into three broad categories:
        1. policy iteration methods, which alternate between estimating the value function under the current
           policy and improving the policy
        2. policy gradient methods, which use an estimator of the gradient of the expected return (total
           reward) obtained from sample trajectories (and which, as we later discuss, have a close connection
           to policy iteration)
        3. derivative-free optimization methods, such as the cross-entropy method (CEM) and covariance matrix
           adaptation (CMA), which treat the return as a black box function to be optimized in terms of the
           policy parameters

        
        In this article, we first prove that minimizing a certain surrogate objective function guarantees
        policy improvement with non-trivial step sizes. Then we make a series of approximations to the
        theoretically-justified algorithm, yielding a practical algorithm, which we call trust region policy
        optimization (TRPO).

        
* Preliminaries
        Consider an infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision process (MDP), defined by the tuple
        \((\cals,\cala,P,r,\rho_0,\gamma)\), where \(\cals\) is a finite set of states, \(\cala\) is a finite set of actions,
        \(P:\cals\times\cala\times\cals\to\R\) is the transition probability distribution, \(r:\cals\to\R\) is the reward function,
        \(\rho_0:\cals\to\R\) is the distribution of the initial state \(s_0\), and \(\gamma\in(0,1)\) is the discount factor.

        #+LATEX: \wu{
        "infinite-horizon": allow infinite interaction

        "discounted": future rewards are worth less than immediate rewards. The return function at time \(t\) is
        \begin{equation*}
        G_t=r_t+\gamma r_{t+1}+\gamma^2r_{t+2}+\gamma^3r_{t+3}+\cdots
        \end{equation*}
        #+LATEX: }

        Let
        \(\pi\)
        denote a stochastic policy \(\pi:\cals\times\cala\to[0,1]\), and let \(\eta(\pi)\) denote its expected discounted reward:
        \begin{equation*}
        \eta(\pi)=\E_{s_0,a_0,\dots}\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tr(s_t)\right]
        \end{equation*}
        where
        \begin{equation*}
        s_0\sim\rho_0(s_0),a_t\sim\pi(a_t|s_t),s_{t+1}\sim P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)
        \end{equation*}

        We will use the following standard definitions for *state-action value function* \(Q_\pi\), the *value function*
        \(V_{\pi}\) and the *advantage function* \(A_\pi\)
        \begin{align*}
        &Q_\pi(s_t,a_t)=\E_{s_{t+1},a_{t+1},\dots}\left[\sum_{l=0}^\infty\gamma^lr(s_{t+l})\right]\\
        &V_\pi(s_t)=\E_{a_t,s_{t+1},\dots}\left[\sum_{l=0}^\infty\gamma^lr(s_{t+l})\right]\\
        &A_\pi(s,a)=Q_\pi(s,a)-V_\pi(s)\text{ where }\\
        &\quad\quad a_t\sim\pi(a_t|s_t),s_{t+1}\sim P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)\text{ for }t\ge 0
        \end{align*}

        The following useful identity expresses the expected return of another policy \(\hat{\pi}\) in terms of the
        advantage over \(\pi\), accumulated over timesteps
        \begin{equation*}
        \eta(\tilde{\pi})=\eta(\pi)+\E_{s_0,a_0,\dots\sim\tilde{\pi}}\left[\sum_{i=0}^\infty\gamma^tA_\pi(s_t,a_t)\right]
        \end{equation*}

        Let \(\rho_\pi\) be the *(unnormalized) discounted visitation frequencies*:
        \begin{equation*}
        \rho_\pi(s)=P(s_0=s)+\gamma P(s_1=s)+\gamma^2P(s_2=s)+\dots
        \end{equation*}
        where \(s_0\sim\rho_0\) and the actions are chosen accoding to \(\pi\).

        #+LATEX: \wu{
        Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are two different random variables. Then
        \begin{align*}
        \E[X+Y]&=\sum_x\sum_y (x+y)p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\sum_x\sum_y  xp_{X,Y}(x,y)+\sum_x\sum_yyp_{X,Y}(x,y)\\
        &=\sum_xxP(X=x)+\sum_yyP(Y=y)\\
        &=\E[X]+\E[Y]
        \end{align*}
        #+LATEX: }

        We can rewrite \(\eta(\tilde{\pi})\) as
        \begin{align}
        \eta(\tilde{\pi})&=\eta(\pi)+\sum_{t=0}^\infty\sum_sP(s_t=s|\tilde{\pi})\sum_a\tilde{\pi}(a|s)\gamma^tA_\pi(s,a)\notag\\
        &=\eta(\pi)+\sum_s\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tP(s_t=s|\tilde{\pi})\sum_a\tilde{\pi}(a|s)A_\pi(s,a)\notag\\
        &=\eta(\pi)+\sum_s\rho_{\tilde{\pi}}(s)\sum_{a}\tilde{\pi}(a|s)A_\pi(s,a)\label{eq2}
        \end{align}
        This equation implies that any policy update \(\pi\to\tilde{\pi}\) that has a nonnegative expected advantage at *every*
        state \(s\), i.e., \(\sum_a\tilde{\pi}(a|s)A_\pi(s,a)\ge 0\), is guaranteed to increase the policy performance \(\eta\), or leave
        it constant in the case that the expected advantage is zero everywhere.

        This implies the classic result that the update performed by exact policy iteration, which uses the
        deterministic policy
        \begin{equation*}
        \tilde{\pi}(s)=\argmax_aA_\pi(s,a),
        \end{equation*}
        
        improves the policy if there is at least one state-action pair with a positive advantage value and
        nonzero state visitation probability, otherwise the algorithm has converged to the optimal policy.

        However, in the approximate setting, it will typically be unavoidable, due to estimation and
        approximation error, that there will be some states \(s\) for which the expected advantage is negative,
        that is, \(\sum_a\tilde{\pi}(a|s)A_\pi(s,a)<0\). The complex dependency of \(\rho_{\tilde{\pi}}(s)\) on \(\tilde{\pi}\) makes Equation [[eqref:eq2]]  difficult to
        optimize directly. Instead, we introduce the following local approximation to \(\eta\):
        \begin{equation*}
        L_\pi(\tilde{\pi})=\eta(\pi)+\sum_s\rho_\pi(s)\sum_a\tilde{\pi}(a|s)A_\pi(s,a)
        \end{equation*}

        If we have a parameterized policy \(\pi_\theta\), where \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) is a differentiable function of the parameter vector
        \(\theta\), then \(L_\pi\) matches \(\eta\) to first order. That is
        \begin{gather}
        L_{\pi_{\theta_0}}(\pi_{\theta_0})=\eta(\pi_{\theta_0})\notag\\
        \nabla_\theta L_{\pi_{\theta_0}}(\pi_\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_0}=\nabla_\theta\eta(\pi_\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_0}\label{eq4}
        \end{gather}

        Equation [[eqref:eq4]] implies that a sufficiently small step \(\pi_{\theta_0}\to\tilde{\pi}\) that improves
        \(L_{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}\) will also improve \(\eta\), but does not give us any guidance on how big of a step to take

        To address this issue, [[cite:&645531.656005]] proposed a policy updating scheme called *convervative
        policy interation*, for which they could provide explicit lower bounds on the improvement of \(\eta\). To
        define the conservative policy interation update, let \(\pi_{old}\) denote the current policy, and let
        \(\pi'=\argmax_{\pi'}L_{\pi_{old}}(\pi')\). The new policy \(\pi_{new}\) was defined to be the following mixture
        \begin{equation*}
        \pi_{new}(a|s)=(1-\alpha)\pi_{old}(a|s)+\alpha\pi'(a|s)
        \end{equation*}
        And we have the following lower bound
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq6}
        \eta(\pi_{new})>L_{\pi_{old}}(\pi_{new})-\frac{2\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\alpha^2
        \end{equation}
        where \(\epsilon=\max_s\abs{\E_{a\sim\pi'(a|s)}[A_\pi(s,a)]}\)


* Monotonic Improvement Guarantee for General Stochastic Policies
        Our result is that the policy improvement bound in Equation [[eqref:eq6]] can be extended to general
        stochastic policies.

        The particular distance measure we use is the total variation divergence, which is defined by
        \(D_{TV}(p\parallel q)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_i\abs{p_i-q_i}\) for discrete probability densitiy \(p,q\). Define
        \begin{equation*}
        D_{TV}^{max}(\pi,\tilde{\pi})=\max_sD_{TV}(\pi(\cdot|s)\parallel \tilde{\pi}(\cdot|s))
        \end{equation*}

        #+ATTR_LATEX: :options []
        #+BEGIN_theorem
        [[label:thm1]]
        Let \(\alpha=D_{TV}^{max}(\pi_{old},\pi_{new})\). Then the following bound holds
        \begin{equation*}
        \eta(\pi_{new})\ge L_{\pi_{old}}(\pi_{new})-\frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma^2)}\alpha^2
        \end{equation*}
        where \(\epsilon=\max_{s,a}\abs{A_{\pi_{old}}(s,a)}\)
        #+END_theorem

        Next, we note the following relationship between the total variation divergence and the KL divergence
        \begin{equation*}
        D_{TV}(p\parallel q)^2\le D_{KL}(p\parallel q)
        \end{equation*}
        #+LATEX: \wu{
        \begin{equation*}
        D_{KL}(p\parallel q)=\sum_xp(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
        \end{equation*}
        #+LATEX: }
        
        Let \(D_{KL}^{max}(\pi,\tilde{\pi})=\max_sD_{KL}(\pi(\cdot|s)\parallel \tilde{\pi}(\cdot|s))\). The following bound follows from Theorem [[ref:thm1]]
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq9}
        \eta(\tilde{\pi})\ge L_\pi(\tilde{\pi})-CD^{max}_{KL}(\pi,\tilde{\pi})
        \end{equation}
        where \(C=\frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\)

        It follows from Equation [[eqref:eq9]] that Algorithm ref:alg1 is guaranteed to generate a monotonically
        improving sequence of policies \(\eta(\pi_0)\le\eta(\pi_1)\le\dots\).

        To see this, let \(M_i(\pi)=L_{\pi_i}(\pi)-CD_{KL}^{max}(\pi_i,\pi)\). Then
        1. \(\eta(\pi_{i+1})\ge M_i(\pi_{i+1})\) by Equation eqref:eq9
        2. \(\eta(\pi_i)=M_i(\pi_i)\)
        3. \(\eta(\pi_{i+1})-\eta(\pi_i)\ge M_i(\pi_{i+1})-M_i(\pi_i)\)

* Optimization of Parameterized Policies
        We now describe how to derive a practical algorithm from these theoretical foundations, under finite
        sample counts and arbitrary parameterizations.

        Since we consider parameterized policies \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) with parameter vector \(\theta\), we will overload our previous
        notation to use functions of \(\theta\) rather than \(\pi\), e.g., \(\eta(\theta):=\eta(\pi_\theta)\),
        \(L_\theta(\tiltheta):=L_{\pi_\theta}(\pi_{\tiltheta})\), and \(D_{KL}(\theta\parallel\tiltheta):=D_{KL}(\pi_\theta\parallel\pi_{\tilde{\pi}})\)

        The preceding section showed that \(\eta(\theta)\ge L_{\theta_{old}}(\theta)-CD_{KL}^{max}(\theta_{old},\theta)\)
        with equality at \(\theta=\theta_{old}\). Thus by performing the following maximization, we are guaranteed to improve
        the true objective \(\eta\):
        \begin{equation*}
        \text{maximize}_\theta[L_{\theta_{old}}(\theta)-CD_{KL}^{max}(\theta_{old},\theta)]
        \end{equation*}
        
        In practice, if we used the penalty coefficient C recommended by the theory above, the step sizes
        would be very small. One way to take larger steps in a robust way is to use a constraint on the KL
        divergence between the new policy and the old policy, i.e., a trust region constraint:
        \begin{align*}
        &\text{maximize}_\theta L_{\theta_{old}}(\theta)\\
        &\text{subject to }D_{KL}^{max}(\theta_{old},\theta)\le\delta
        \end{align*}
        We can use a heuristic approximation which considers the average KL divergence
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq12}
        \barD^\rho_{KL}(\theta_1,\theta_2):=\E_{s\sim\rho}[D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_1}(\cdot|s)\parallel\pi_{\theta_2}(\cdot|s))]
        \end{equation}
        
        

* Sample-Based Estimation of the Objective and Constraint
        The previous section proposed a constrained optimization problem on the policy parameters (Equation
        [[eqref:eq12]], which optimizes an estimate of the expected total reward \(\eta\) subject to a constraint on the
        change in the policy at each update. This section describes how the objective and constraint functions
        can be approximated using Monte Carlo simulation.

        We seek to solve the following optimization problem, obtained by expanding \(L_{\theta_{old}}\) in Equation [[eqref:eq12]]
        \begin{align}
        &\text{maximize}_\theta\sum_s\rho_{\theta_{old}}(s)\sum_a\pi_\theta(a|s)A_{\theta_{old}}(s,a)\label{eq13}\\
        &\text{subject to }\barD_{KL}^{\rho_{\theta_{old}}}(\theta_{old},\theta)\le\delta\notag
        \end{align}

        We first replace \(\sum_s\rho_{\theta_{old}}(s)[\dots]\) by the expectation \(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\E_{s\sim\rho_{\theta_{old}}}\). Next we replace the advantage values
        \(A_{\theta_{old}}\) by the \(Q\)-values \(Q_{\theta_{old}}\), which only changes the objective by a constant. Last, we replace the sum
        over the actions by an importance sampling estimator. Using \(q\) to denote the sampling distribution, the
        contribution of a single \(s_n\) to the loss function is
        \begin{equation*}
        \sum_a\pi_\theta(a|s_n)A_{\theta_{old}}(s_n,a)=\E_{a\sim q}
        \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s_n)}{q(a|s_n)}A_{\theta_{old}}(s_n,a) \right]
        \end{equation*}
        Our opimization problem in Equation [[eqref:eq13]] is exactly equivalent to the following one, written in
        terms of expectations:
        \begin{align}
        &\text{maximize}_\theta\E_{s\sim\rho_{\theta_{old}},a\sim q}\left[
        \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{q(a|s)}A_{\theta_{old}}(s_n,a) \right]\label{eq14}\\
        &\text{subject to }\E_{s\sim\rho_{\theta_{old}}}[D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s)\parallel\pi_\theta(\cdot|s))]
        \le\delta\notag
        \end{align}

        All that remains is to replace the expectations by sample averages and replace the \(Q\) value by an
        empirical estimate. The following sections describe two different schemes for performing this estimation.

** Single Path
        In this estimation procedure, we collect a sequence of states by sampling \(s_0\sim\rho_0\) and then simulating the
        policy \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) for some number of timesteps to generate a trajectory \(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots,s_{T-1},a_{T-1},s_T\). Hence,
        \(q(a|s)=\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)\). \(Q_{\theta_{old}}(s,a)\) is computed at each state-action pair \((s_t,a_t)\) by taking the discounted
        sum of future rewards along the trajectory.

** Vine
        In this estimation procedure, we first sample \(s_0\sim\rho_0\) and simulate the policy \(\pi_{\theta_i}\) to
        generate a number of trajectories. We then choose a subset of \(N\) states along these trajectories,
        denoted \(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_N\) , which we call the “rollout set”. For each state \(s_n\) in the rollout set, we sample
        \(K\) actions according to \(a_{n,k}\sim q(\cdot|s_n)\). Any choice of \(q(\cdot|s_n)\) with a support that includes the support of
        \(\pi_{\theta_i}(\cdot|s_n)\) will produce a consistent estimator.
        #+LATEX: \wu{
        Let \((X,\mathcal T)\) be a topological space, and let \(\mu\) be a probability measure on \(X\). The support of \(\mu\) is:
        \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname{supp}(\mu)=\left\{x\in X:\mu(U)>0\text{ for every open set }U\ni x\right\}
        \end{equation*}

        For a real-valued \(X\), “every open neighborhood” can be written as every open interval:
        \begin{equation*}
        x\in\operatorname{supp}(X)\iff P(X\in(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon))>0\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.
        \end{equation*}
        #+LATEX: }

        In practice, we found that \(q(\cdot|s_n)=\pi_{\theta_i}(\cdot|s_n)\) works well on continuous problems, such as robotic
        locomotion, while the uniform distribution works well on discrete tasks, such as the Atari games,
        where it can sometimes achieve better exploration.

        For each action \(a_{n,k}\) sampled at each state \(s_n\), we estimate \(\hatQ_{\theta_i}(s_n,a_n,k)\) by performing a rollout (i.e., a
        short trajectory) starting with state \(s_n\) and action \(a_{n,k}\) . We can greatly reduce the variance of the
        \(Q\)-value differences between rollouts by using the same random number sequence for the noise in each
        of the \(K\) rollouts, i.e., common random numbers.

        In small, finite action spaces, we can generate a rollout for every possible action from a given
        state. The contribution to \(L_{\theta_{old}}\) from a single state \(s_n\) is as follows:
        \begin{equation*}
        L_n(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_\theta(a_k|s_n)\hatQ(s_n,a_k)
        \end{equation*}
        where the action space is \(\cala=\{a_1,\dots,a_K\}\).

        In large or continuous state spaces, we can construct an estimator of the surrogate objective using
        importance sampling. The self-normalized estimator of \(L_{\theta_{old}}\) obtained at a single state \(s_n\) is
        \begin{equation*}
        L_n(\theta)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\pi_\theta(a_{n,k}|s_n)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_{n,k}|s_n)}\hatQ(s_n,a_{n,k})}
        {\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\pi_\theta(a_{n,k}|s_n)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_{n,k}|s_n)}}
        \end{equation*}
        assuming that we performed \(K\) actions \(a_{n,1},a_{n,2},\dots,a_{n,K}\) from state \(s_n\).
        This self-normalized estimator removes the need to use a baseline for the \(Q\)-values. Averaging over
        \(s_n\sim\rho(\pi)\), we obtain an estimator for \(L_{\theta_{old}}\), as well as its gradient.

        #+LATEX: \wu{
        Note that
        \begin{equation*}
        \mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_{\rm old}}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\rm old}(a\mid s)}\right]=1
        \end{equation*}
        since
        \begin{equation*}
        \E_{a\sim\pi_{old}}\left[ \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{old}(a|s)} \right]=\sum_a\pi_{old}(a|s)\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{old}(a|s)}
        =\sum_a\pi_\theta(a|s)=1
        \end{equation*}

        Self-normalized estimator is a standard monte carlo technique.

* Practical Algorithm
        1. Use the /single path/ or /vine procedures/ to collect a set of state-action pairs along with Monte
           Carlo estimates of their \(Q\)-values.
        2. By averaging over samples, construct the estimated objective and constraint in Equation [[eqref:eq14]].
        3. Approximately solve this constrained optimization problem to update the policy’s parameter vector
           \(\theta\). We use the conjugate gradient algorithm followed by a line search, which is altogether only
           slightly more expensive than computing the gradient itself

        #+BEGIN_lemma
Given two policies \(\pi\), \(\tilde{\pi}\)
\begin{equation*}
\eta(\tilde{\pi})=\eta(\pi)+\E_{\tau\sim\tilde{\pi}}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tA_\pi(s_t,a_t) \right]
\end{equation*}
This expectation is taken over trajectories \(\tau:=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots)\), and the notation \(\E_{\tau\sim\tilde{\pi}[\dots]}\)
indicates actions are sampled from \(\tilde{\pi}\) to generate \(\tau\)
        #+END_lemma

        #+BEGIN_proof
        First note that \(A_\pi(s,a)=\E_{s'\sim P(s'|s,a)}[r(s)+\gamma V_\pi(s')-V_\pi(s)]\).
        \begin{align*}
        &\mathbb{E}_{\tau|\tilde{\pi}}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tA_\pi(s_t,a_t) \right]\\
        &=\mathbb{E}_{\tau|\tilde{\pi}}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t(r(s_t)+\gamma V_\pi(s_{t+1})-V_\pi(s_t)) \right]\\
        &=\mathbb{E}_{\tau|\tilde{\pi}}\left[ -V_\pi(s_0)+\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tr(s_t) \right]\\
        &=-\mathbb{E}_{s_0}[V_\pi(s_0)]+\mathbb{E}_{\tau|\tilde{\pi}}\left[ \sum_{i=0}^\infty\gamma^tr(s_t) \right]\\
        &=-\eta(\pi)+\eta(\tilde{\pi})
        \end{align*}
        #+END_proof

** Efficiently Solving the Trust-Region Constrained Optimization Problem
        Solve
        \begin{equation*}
        \text{maximize }L(\theta)\text{ subject to }\barD_{KL}(\theta_{old},\theta)\le\delta
        \end{equation*}
        1. compute a search direction, using a linear approximation to objective and quadratic approximation
           to the constraint
        2. perform a line search in that direction, ensuring that we improve the nonlinear objective while
           satisfying the nonlinear constraint.

        The search direction is computed by approximately solving the equation \(Ax=g\), where \(A\) is the Fisher
        information matrix, i.e., the quadratic approximation to the KL divergence constraint:
        \(\barD_{KL}(\theta_{old},\theta)\approx\frac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta_{old})^TA(\theta-\theta_{old})\), where
        \(A_{ij}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j}\barD_{KL}(\theta_{old},\theta)\). In large-scale problems, it is prohibitively costly (with respect to computation
        and memory) to form the full matrix \(A\) (or \(A^{-1}\)). However, the conjugate gradient algorithm allows us
        to approximately solve the equation \(Ax=b\) without forming this full matrix, when we merely have
        access to a function that computes matrix-vector products \(y\to Ay\)

*** Computing the Fisher-Vector Product
        Here we will describe how to compute the matrix-vector product between the averaged Fisher information
        matrix and arbitrary vectors. This matrix-vector product enables us to perform the conjugate gradient
        algorithm.

        Suppose that the parameterized policy maps from the input \(x\) to “distribution parameter”
        vector \(\mu_\theta(x)\), which parameterizes the distribution \(\pi(u|x)\). Now the KL divergence for a given input
        \(x\) can be written as follows:
        \begin{equation*}
        D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|x)\parallel\pi_\theta(\cdot|x))=kl(\mu_\theta(x),\mu_{old}(x))
        \end{equation*}
        where kl is the KL divergence between the distributions corresponding to the two mean parameter
        vectors.

        #+LATEX: \wu{
        For example, guassian distribution needs two parameter "mean" and "log(standard deviation)"

        \(\mu_\theta(x)=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k)\) and \(\mu_a(x)\) is the \(a\)-th parameter of \(\mu_\theta(x)\).
        #+LATEX: }

        Differentiating \(kl\) twice w.r.t. \(\theta\), we obtain
        \begin{equation*}
       \frac{\partial\mu_a(x)}{\partial\theta_i}\frac{\partial\mu_b(x)}{\partial\theta_j}kl''_{ab}(\mu_\theta(x),\mu_{old})+
        \frac{\partial^2\mu_a(x)}{\partial\theta_i\partial\theta_j}kl'_a(\mu_\theta(x),\mu_{old})
        \end{equation*}
        , and there is an implied summation over indices \(a,b\).
